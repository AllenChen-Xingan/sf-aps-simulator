When does money flow to accessible signals?

An agent-based simulator of San Francisco's request list for Accessible Pedestrian Signals (APS) — the push-button signals that tell blind and low-vision pedestrians, by sound and vibration, when it is safe to cross.

What this is

San Francisco installs APS every year, but residents who ask for one at a specific intersection join a list, and many of those requests wait for years. In the city's records, a requested intersection gets APS after it is funded. Who prepares the request for money, and under which rules can it succeed?

This page lets you test answers to that question. It simulates the people and offices involved — residents, advocates, SFMTA engineers, SFMTA's grants staff, the county transportation authority, Caltrans — month by month for ten years from July 2026. You pick a policy pathway, press Run, and watch who acts, where money moves, and which intersections get installed. Underneath, the same model was run tens of thousands of times with uncertain inputs drawn at random, so you can see how often each pathway works.

Use it to compare policy designs, not to predict numbers. The rules come from public documents and an interview with SFMTA staff, but many values are informed guesses (listed under Method and limits).

    How to read this

    • One run is one possible story. Change the seed and the same settings tell a different story. Compare pathways on the many-run tables, not on one animation.
    • Rankings and conditions are the output; counts are illustrations. “Pathway X beat the status quo in 9 of 10 parameter sets” is the kind of statement the model supports. “X will install 27 signals” is not.
    • The rules are set by people. If a rule is wrong, the result is wrong. Each rule and range is listed with its source in the design document.
    • Dashed links in the network are steps that the rules allow but that did not happen in this run, such as the request list turning into an application for money.
    • Mechanisms are competing explanations of why funded requests stopped. Running a pathway under each one shows which recommendations hold up whichever explanation is right.

    Method and limits

    Model in brief (ODD summary)
    Purpose
    Explore under which conditions someone applies for money for the APS request list and gets it, and which policy pathways are robust across many uncertain futures.
    Entities
    Intersections on the request list (76 pending in mid-2026 plus new requests); one behavioural actor — SFMTA's capital budget and grants staff, who decide whether to prepare an application; rule-following actors: SFMTA APS engineering, the Signal Shop crews, advocates, external enforcement; venues: the federal Highway Safety Improvement Program (HSIP) run by Caltrans, the county sales-tax program (Prop L) run by SFCTA, the city General Fund, the federal Safe Streets for All grants, and signal projects that carry APS along.
    Schedule
    Monthly steps from July 2026 for ten years. Each month: new requests arrive and are classified; advocates may raise the issue, which may be deflected; the grants staff may start, finish or miss an application; venues decide on their own calendars; funded sites are installed by crews or with signal projects.
    Key rules
    The chance that an application is started each month depends on whether there is a named owner, a written deadline, oversight, recent advocacy pressure and external enforcement. HSIP applications must reach a benefit–cost ratio of 5.0 from injury crashes and crash-reduction factors, and must clear that cycle's cut-off; APS cannot be funded alone and must ride on a countermeasure such as signal-hardware upgrades. Delivery milestones and failure paths (missed deadlines, red flags, cost overruns, staff leaving, vetoes) are modelled explicitly.
    Pathways
    Adaptive: each starts with one action and switches to a fallback when a signpost is crossed (Haasnoot et al., 2013).
    Analysis
    Latin-hypercube draws of the uncertain inputs, several replicates per draw, the same draws and seeds for every pathway and mechanism. Planned for the full analysis: fitting to 2022–2025 snapshots, held-out checks, a zero-effect control, and rule-finding (PRIM) on held-out runs.
    Calibration and validation status

    Not yet calibrated. The full analysis plan fits each mechanism to the 2022–2025 list snapshots and checks it against later snapshots held out in advance. Until those results are in, every number here comes from uncalibrated prior ranges.

    Limits
    • Scenario exploration, not prediction. The model cannot say what will happen, only what follows from its rules under many assumptions.
    • The rules are set by people from documents and one interview. Behaviour inside agencies is simplified to one decision: whether to prepare an application.
    • Many ranges are informed guesses, including how much a named owner or advocacy pressure raises the chance of applying. A version with these effects set to zero is part of the full analysis.
    • Only intersections on the request list are modelled, not city-wide coverage, and only San Francisco.
    • Crash data cover injury crashes within 50 m; property-damage-only crashes are missing, so safety benefits are understated.
    • Fitting the history rests on very few funding events, so it can rule out little.
    Consistency check

    The browser runs the same Python model file as the offline analysis, through Pyodide (Python compiled to WebAssembly). A test runs all fixed cases in both and compares every output, including every event; the last run found no differences. A second test replays each run's events and checks that the animation's counts equal the model's own counts every month.
