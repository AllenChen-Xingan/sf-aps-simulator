SF APS ABM Model

Blind and low-vision residents of San Francisco can ask the city to install an accessible pedestrian signal (APS) — a crossing button that tells you by sound and vibration when it is safe to walk — at a particular intersection. But a request is only installed after someone gets money for it. This simulation shows how a request moves between residents, the city's transportation agency (SFMTA) and the funders, where it gets stuck, and what changes if the city works differently.

How to watch, in four steps
  1. Pick a pathway — a way the city could change how it funds APS. “PW0 · Status quo” is today.
  2. Press Run. The model plays ten years, month by month, from July 2026.
  3. Watch the network. Links light up when someone acts. The red dashed link — SFMTA's funding staff submitting an application — is the one that usually stays dark. Select any box to see who it is.
  4. Watch the rows below it. Each dot is a requested intersection; dots move down as they get funded and installed.

    Every pathway was run on the same parameter sets with the same random seeds. Pathways draw random events in a different order, so outside shocks are not identical across pathways.

    How the numbers were made: the model's uncertain inputs (costs, chances, delays) were drawn many times; each draw is one parameter set, and each set was run 20 times with different luck.

    • Money within 5 years: in what share of all those runs request-list money arrived within five years.
    • Did better than the status quo: in what share of parameter sets the pathway installed more intersections within ten years than the status quo did with the same set.
    • The same, with the direct start effect set to zero: the same share, after removing the model's assumption that a named owner, a deadline, oversight and advocates' pressure each make SFMTA staff more likely to start an application. Outside enforcement (negotiation, lawsuit) still acts.

    About this model

    What it is

    People who ask San Francisco for an APS at a specific intersection join a request list; in the city's records a requested intersection gets APS after it is funded. The model simulates who prepares requests for money and under which rules they succeed: residents, advocates, SFMTA engineers and grants staff, the county transportation authority (SFCTA), Caltrans and the federal safety grant (HSIP), and city crews. It runs month by month for ten years from July 2026.

    How to read it

    • One run is one possible story; a new seed tells another. Compare pathways on the many-run table, not on one animation.
    • Rankings and conditions are the output; counts are illustrations.
    • The rules are set by people from public documents and an interview with SFMTA staff. Many ranges are informed guesses. Parameters were screened against the 2022–2025 request lists, but that screen is weak and is not a calibration; results mostly follow the ranges.
    • Pressure from advocates, a named owner and crew capacity are not among the twelve inputs that move results most. That is because the model gives them no channel to act through except the chance of starting an application (or a monthly cap), not because they are unimportant in reality.
    • In the network, the dashed link (grants staff taking up the request list) stays dashed until it happens in the run.

    Limits

    Scenario projections, not forecasts or proof of cause. Request list only, San Francisco only. Injury crashes only, so safety benefits are understated. Behaviour inside agencies is reduced to one decision: whether to prepare an application. PW1's last step (the Director moving money) is a hypothetical fallback whose chance and amount are assumed.
